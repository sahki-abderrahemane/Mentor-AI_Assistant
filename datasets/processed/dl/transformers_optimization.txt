Introduction
Modern large language models (LLMs) derive their power from a combination
of architectural routing choices, efficient adaptation strategies, tokenization
granularity, and inference-time optimizations.
Each of these components
has a deep mathematical foundation, and their interaction governs the per-
formance, memory footprint, and deployability of models with billions of
parameters.
This report provides a rigorous end-to-end analysis of these
interconnected systems.
We begin by formalizing Transformer attention as a graph routing prob-
lem.
The attention mask acts as an adjacency matrix that defines valid
edges in a fully connected graph of token nodes.
We compare the three
dominant routing paradigms: the open mask of BERT for bidirectional un-
derstanding, the causal mask of GPT for autoregressive generation, and the
encoder-decoder cross-attention of T5 for conditional generation. A complete
mathematical proof demonstrates why additive −∞masking forces softmax
attention weights to exactly zero on illegal edges, preventing information
leakage. PyTorch implementations are provided throughout.
The second section tackles parameter-efficient fine-tuning. We derive the
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) decomposition W0 + BA from the intrinsic
rank hypothesis, and explain the critical initialization strategy (A random
with Kaiming uniform, B exactly zero) with a gradient-flow analysis showing
what happens when this configuration is inverted. We then extend the discus-
sion to QLoRA, which quantizes frozen weights to 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4)
and applies double quantization to scaling constants, enabling fine-tuning of
65B-parameter models on a single GPU.
The final section addresses tokenization internals and inference engineer-
ing. We contrast Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), WordPiece, and SentencePiece,
with particular attention to byte-level fallback and its disproportionate im-
pact on non-Latin scripts. We then derive the KV-cache mechanism math-
ematically, showing how caching past key and value projections transforms
autoregressive generation from an O(T 2) compute bottleneck to an O(1)
per-step operation at the cost of linear memory growth.
Together, these
topics constitute the engineering backbone of the modern LLM lifecycle.

T nodes, each representing a token embedding. The attention mechanism

Comparative Architectural Analysis
2.2.1
BERT: Encoder-Only Bidirectional Mask
BERT employs a zero mask, M = 0.
Every token can attend to every
other token in both directions, allowing the model to build rich bidirectional
representations.
This makes it ideal for classification and understanding
tasks. The absence of autoregressive constraints also permits fully parallel
computation over all positions, making training highly efficient.
2.2.2

2.2.3
T5: Encoder-Decoder Cross-Attention
The T5 model pairs a bidirectional encoder with a causal decoder.
The
encoder uses an all-zero mask, processing the source sequence in parallel.
The decoder uses a causal self-attention mask (like GPT), but additionally
includes a cross-attention sub-layer where the queries come from the decoder
hidden states, and the keys and values come from the encoder output. The
cross-attention mask is fully open (M = 0), allowing every decoder position
to attend to the entire encoded source. This design is powerful for conditional
generation tasks such as translation and summarization.

a product of two low-rank matrices:

Initialization Mechanics: Why A Random, B Zero
To ensure that the fine-tuning starts from the exact pre-trained model, the
adapter contribution must be zero at initialization. This is achieved by set-
ting one of the two matrices to zero. The standard LoRA initialization is:
• A is initialized with nn.init.kaiming uniform to preserve forward-activation
variance,
• B is initialized to 0.

A is frozen at random, effectively learning which directions in the random
projection are useful. Empirically, the standard initialization consistently
yields better results and is universally adopted.
3.3
QLoRA: 4-bit NormalFloat Quantization
QLoRA pushes LoRA’s memory savings further by quantizing the frozen
pre-trained weights to 4 bits. The core innovations are:
NormalFloat4 (NF4). The pre-trained weights are assumed to follow
an approximately normal distribution. The NF4 data type partitions the
standard normal distribution into 24 = 16 equally probable intervals, obtain-
ing the quantile values q0, q1, . . . , q15 of the cumulative distribution function
Φ. A weight w is quantized to the index of the nearest quantile. This min-
imizes information loss compared to uniform quantization when the data is
Gaussian.
Double Quantization (DQ). The per-block scaling constants (one per
small block of weights) are themselves quantized to 8-bit floats, further re-
ducing the memory footprint.
On-the-fly dequantization. During the forward and backward passes,
the stored 4-bit indices are dequantized back to FP16 as needed:
wFP16 = s · (NF4 table[idx]),
(7)
8

where s is the block scaling factor.
This dequantization happens in the
GPU’s fast memory, but the arithmetic is still performed in FP16.
The
main bottleneck is the additional memory bandwidth and compute spent on
reading and decompressing the 4-bit representations. Despite this overhead,
QLoRA enables fine-tuning a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU.

Tokenization Internals & Inference Engi-

4.1
Subword Tokenization Algorithms
Tokenization converts raw text into a sequence of integers. Modern LLMs rely
on subword algorithms that balance vocabulary size and out-of-vocabulary
(OOV) handling. The choice of tokenizer has profound implications for se-
quence length, model efficiency, and multilingual performance.
4.1.1
Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)
BPE starts with a character-level vocabulary and iteratively merges the most
frequent pair of adjacent symbols. Merging is guided solely by co-occurrence
frequency. It is used by GPT models. The vocabulary is deterministic given a
training corpus and the number of merge operations. BPE has the advantage
of simplicity and handles rare words by splitting them into frequent subword
units.
4.1.2
WordPiece
Originally proposed for BERT, WordPiece also builds a subword vocabulary
by merging pairs, but the criterion is based on likelihood maximization: at
each step, the pair (a, b) that maximizes the increase in the training data
likelihood under the current language model is merged. This tends to pro-
duce a vocabulary that is more linguistically coherent, but requires a more
expensive training procedure. The likelihood objective is:
score(a, b) =
count(a, b)
count(a) · count(b),
(8)
where higher scores indicate pairs that occur together more often than ex-
pected by chance.
4.1.3
SentencePiece
SentencePiece treats the input as a raw byte stream, completely bypassing
language-specific pre-tokenization (e.g., splitting on spaces). It implements
10

both BPE and unigram language model tokenization. By operating directly
on Unicode bytes, it ensures a lossless tokenization that can handle any
language, even those without explicit word boundaries. A key component is
byte-level fallback: if a token is not in the vocabulary, it is split into UTF-8
bytes and represented by special byte tokens. This eliminates OOV entirely
but can dramatically increase sequence lengths for scripts like Chinese, Ko-
rean, or Arabic, where characters may decompose into multiple bytes.

KV-Cache Dynamics in Autoregressive Generation
Autoregressive generation produces tokens one by one.
Without caching,
at step t the Transformer recomputes keys and values for all previous t −1
tokens, leading to a total cost proportional to O(T 2).
The KV-cache stores the key and value projections of all processed
tokens. At step t, only the new token’s query, key, and value are computed.
The attention then uses the cached K1:t−1 and V1:t−1:
at = softmax
qt[K1:t−1; kt]⊤
√dk

[V1:t−1; vt].

The computation per new token becomes O(t) (since we multiply a single
query against t keys), but the matrix multiplication is dominated by a small
constant factor. Moreover, the compute is independent of the number of
layers, and each step adds a constant-sized new kt, vt to the cache. Thus
the per-step compute is effectively O(1) in terms of scaling with t, though
the memory footprint grows linearly with the sequence length T, as we must
store two matrices of size (T, dmodel) per layer.

Conclusion
This report has dissected the key engineering and mathematical components
that underpin modern Transformers. The architectural routing choices—bidirectional
12

(BERT), causal (GPT), or encoder-decoder (T5)—define the fundamental
capabilities and limitations of a model. Parameter-efficient techniques like
LoRA and QLoRA capitalize on the low intrinsic rank of weight updates,
drastically cutting fine-tuning costs while maintaining performance.
Tok-
enization granularity, governed by BPE, WordPiece, or SentencePiece, crit-
ically impacts sequence length and model compactness. Finally, inference
optimizations such as the KV-cache transform the autoregressive generation
from a quadratic bottleneck into a linear-memory, constant-compute step.
The interaction of these factors shapes the entire LLM lifecycle, from train-
ing to deployment.
